Open GitHub tickets

A point-in-time snapshot of every open issue on Firstmate’s GitHub repository, sorted so a successor can learn from what still goes wrong and what its users keep asking for.

Snapshot method

Captured 2026-09-23 with gh-axi issue list -R kunchenguid/firstmate --state open --limit 1000 --fields labels,body against kunchenguid/firstmate, which reported 563 open issues at that moment. Every issue’s number, title, author, opening age, label and full body were pulled in that one call; nothing here was re-fetched afterward, so a ticket someone closes or relabels after this date will still show as it was captured. Opening dates are approximate: the query reports age as a relative offset (“25d ago”, “2mo ago”), not a timestamp, so the date column below is that offset subtracted from the capture instant, not the ticket’s literal creation timestamp.

Headline findings

  • 488 of 563 open tickets (86%) read as defects — a behaviour gap against what the code or its own docs promise — against only 71 (12%) feature requests and 4 open usage questions. This is a mature system whose open backlog is dominated by correctness reports, not by unmet asks.
  • 370 tickets (65%) already carry a ready-for-pr label and a further 22 carry existing-pr: roughly two in three open tickets already have a proposed fix waiting, so the bottleneck reads as review and merge throughput rather than a shortage of diagnosis or authored fixes.
  • The two largest categories, worktree and task lifecycle and backends and terminals, together account for 215 tickets — 38% of everything open. Keeping a task's worktree and its backend terminal adapter honest under concurrency and restarts is where this system spends most of its open trouble.
  • 14 tickets are flagged on their category page as a design flaw rather than a coding mistake — the mechanism does exactly what it was built to do, and that is the bug. See themes for the full list; this is the most valuable signal for a from-scratch successor.
  • Ages cluster tightly: 461 of 563 tickets (81%) were opened one week to three months before capture, with almost nothing older than six months — consistent with an actively triaged tracker rather than a graveyard of stale reports.

Taxonomy and how it was built

Categories were derived from the tickets, not imposed up front: every title was read, a first pass clustered recurring subsystem language (file names, script names, mechanism names such as “composer”, “lease”, “dispatch”), and the clusters were then named and re-checked against the tickets that landed in them. Two categories (a documentation/usage-question cluster and a third-party-forge cluster) were merged into one because both were small and both were really about the system's edges rather than its internals. The result is 18 categories; each ticket sits in exactly one, chosen for the most specific subsystem it actually concerns.

Within a category, every ticket is tagged as exactly one of:

Defect
The code, or the system's own documented contract, does not do what it says it does. Includes races, silent data loss, a guard that does not guard, and a script that reports success when it did not succeed.
Feature request
A capability that does not exist is being asked for: a new forge, a new mode, a new adapter, a new report.
Question
A user asking whether something is possible or in scope, with no claim that anything is broken.

A ticket also carries a design flaw tag on its category page where reading it shows the problem is architectural — the implementation matches its contract, and the contract itself is what needs to change (for example a safety lease only one of two writers is required to hold, or a merge gate that treats an absent check the same as a passing one). This is distinct from, and rarer than, an ordinary defect.

Classification came from reading each ticket's title and full body, not the title alone: titles here are precise engineering prose (several in German or Dutch), so a title lacking an obvious keyword like “bug” or “feature” was resolved by what the body actually described. Every one of the 563 tickets was read at least at title-plus-opening-paragraph depth; a keyword-scored first pass was checked and hand-corrected wherever the score was ambiguous or the ticket fell outside every category's keyword set, which is the large majority of the work behind this snapshot.
